## The Real Problem With Concrete

First, I want to be fair. Concrete has its place. I use it structurally all the time — footings, retaining wall bases, the substrate under a flagstone installation. It's a workhorse material and I respect it for that.

But as a *finish surface* — the thing you're actually walking on, sitting next to, looking at every day — it almost always disappoints. Here's why:

- **It reflects heat.** Southern California summers are brutal, and a broad concrete surface acts like a storage heater. It absorbs heat all day and radiates it back at you in the evening, right when you want to be outside.
- **It cracks.** Not if. When. Our soil moves, roots grow, and concrete doesn't flex. You get the spiderweb, the heave, the ugly repair patch that never quite matches.
- **It stains.** Leaves, rust, oil, fertilizer, hard water — concrete holds onto all of it. The surface becomes a record of everything that's ever touched it.
- **It sounds wrong underfoot.** This one sounds strange until you've experienced the difference. Walk across a flagstone path barefoot versus a concrete slab. One feels connected to the earth. One feels like a warehouse floor.
- **It looks manufactured.** And in a landscape that's trying to feel natural, organic, and alive — manufactured is exactly what you don't want.

## What I Use Instead

### Decomposed Granite (DG)

For paths, casual entertaining areas, and anything that needs to feel soft and earthy, stabilized decomposed granite is often my first call. It's a crushed granite aggregate that packs down firm but still looks and feels like the California hills. It comes in warm gold, rusty red, and buff tones that play beautifully with native plantings.

Done right — with a stabilizer mixed in and proper edging to hold it in place — it's durable, permeable, and genuinely beautiful. It crunches a little underfoot in a way I love. It smells like the earth when it rains.

The one honest caveat: it needs maintenance. It can migrate, it can need occasional re-raking or refreshing. But for clients who want a space that feels truly integrated with the land, that tradeoff is worth it every time.

### Flagstone

This is probably what I reach for most often when a client needs a hard, durable surface. Real flagstone — not the thin veneer slabs, not the concrete pavers cast to look like stone — has a weight and permanence to it that concrete can never fake.

I mostly work with:
- **Arizona flagstone** in buff and red tones — warm, earthy, reads like the Southwest in the best possible way
- **Pennsylvania bluestone** when someone wants something cooler and more refined
- **Recycled or reclaimed granite** when budget allows and the aesthetic calls for it

The beauty of flagstone is in the irregularity. Tight-fit flagstone with almost no joints looks elegant and deliberate. Flagstone laid with wider gaps planted with creeping thyme or dymondia creates something that looks like it grew out of the ground. I've seen flagstone paths that have been in the ground for thirty years and they look *better* now than the day they were laid.

That doesn't happen with concrete. Ever.

### Gravel and River Rock

For areas that don't need to bear foot traffic, or for drainage swales and plant beds, crushed gravel or river rock does things concrete simply can't. It allows water to percolate into the soil. It reflects light in a way that feels alive and varied rather than flat. It moves and shifts just slightly, which gives a space a loose, naturalistic quality I love.

I often use pea gravel under and around boulders to give that "dry streambed" effect, or decomposed granite fines around the base of native grasses to mimic what the land looks like naturally in the foothills. Done well, it's invisible design — the kind where the hardscape stops feeling like hardscape at all.

### Permeable Pavers (When the Budget and Site Call for It)

There are situations — driveways, heavy-use entertaining areas, motor courts — where you genuinely need something with the structural integrity of a hard surface. In those cases, I'll often specify permeable concrete pavers or natural stone sets rather than a poured slab.

Permeable pavers allow water to move through the joints rather than sheet off into the storm drain. They're modular, so if something heaves or cracks, you pull the affected pieces and reset them without touching the rest. And they age in a way that feels intentional — the joints green up slightly, the surface develops character.

They cost more than concrete. Significantly more, sometimes. But they also last longer, look better at year ten, and contribute to a healthier water cycle in your soil rather than fighting it.

## The Philosophy Underneath All of This

Here's the thing I keep coming back to: concrete is a material that says *"humans were here and they controlled this."* And sometimes that's the right message. A freeway overpass should say that.

Your backyard shouldn't.

A landscape that's doing its job makes you feel like you've stepped into a place that belongs to the land — that was designed *with* the California environment rather than imposed on top of it. Every surface choice either reinforces that or undermines it.

When I walk onto a property and feel flagstone shifting slightly under my feet, hear the crunch of decomposed granite, see native sedge growing up between the gaps in a stone path — that's a landscape that's *breathing*. It has a quality I can only call aliveness.

Poured concrete gives you none of that. It gives you a surface that will look its best on day one and decline from there.

I'd rather give you something that earns its beauty over time.
