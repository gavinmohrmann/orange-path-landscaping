There's a specific kind of disappointment I've watched homeowners go through, and it stays with me.

They spent real money. They worked with someone. The reveal photos looked great. They sent them to friends, maybe posted them somewhere. And then a few weeks later, they stopped going outside.

Not because anything broke. Not because it was ugly. But because it didn't feel like anything. It didn't pull them out the door in the morning with a cup of coffee. It didn't make them linger. It was a finished product, not a place.

That gap — between what photographs well and what actually feels good to be in — is something I think about constantly. It drives almost every decision I make on a job.

## What a Camera Can't Tell You

A camera flattens. It removes smell, temperature, sound, the feeling of a surface underfoot, the quality of shade. It's a two-dimensional slice of something that's supposed to be experienced with your whole body over time.

This means a garden can score very high on camera and very low in person, and nobody warns you about that before you sign a contract.

Here's what photographs beautifully but often fails you in real life:

- **Symmetrical, highly structured layouts.** Clean lines, mirrored plantings, geometric beds. They read as sophisticated in photos. In person, they can feel rigid and slightly institutional — like you're not allowed to actually relax in them.
- **All-hardscape designs with minimal planting.** Concrete, pavers, maybe a wall or two. Looks modern and clean in a wide shot. Feels hot, loud, and lifeless on a July afternoon in Orange County.
- **Specimen plants staged like furniture.** One perfect agave here, one perfect olive there. Deliberate and artful in a photo. In person, it can feel sparse and disconnected — like the plants are auditioning for something rather than actually living there.
- **Matching, catalog-perfect materials.** When everything comes from the same product line and fits together too neatly, there's no visual tension, no story. The eye has nothing to discover.

None of these are inherently wrong. But they're choices that serve the image more than the experience.

## What Actually Makes You Want to Stay Outside

I've been doing this long enough to know what makes someone linger in a garden. It's not one big dramatic thing. It's a collection of smaller, quieter decisions that add up to a feeling.

**Enclosure.** Humans are wired to feel comfortable with something behind us and an open view in front. A garden that has no sense of enclosure — no canopy, no backdrop, no transition between spaces — feels exposed and restless. We don't relax in the middle of a field. We relax at the edge of it. Mass plantings, a well-placed tree, a low boulder grouping — these create the walls and ceiling of an outdoor room without making it feel closed in.

**Imperfection in the materials.** This one I could talk about for hours. Flagstone with irregular edges and natural variation in color gives your eye something to move across. It holds lichen. It weathers into itself. Concrete pavers are level and consistent and they're kind of dead. Not ugly, just inert. When you're sitting in a space and there's nothing in the hardscape to discover, you feel it without knowing why.

**Sound and movement.** A garden that photographs well is often completely silent. But a garden that feels alive has something moving — grasses catching the afternoon breeze, the papery rustle of a California sycamore overhead, water if you can manage it. Sound is the most underrated element in outdoor design. I've sat in gardens that weren't that visually impressive and felt completely at peace, because there was a low constant rustle around me. Your nervous system responds to that.

**Fragrance at the right moments.** Where are you going to be in the morning? Where do you tend to sit in the evening? Those are the spots for Cleveland sage, for native rosemary, for a well-placed lemon verbena. Not scattered everywhere, not just planted for appearance — placed so you brush against something or catch a scent on the warm air when you're already in a good spot.

**Varied texture underfoot.** A single uniform paving surface from the back door to the far wall is efficient and completely uninteresting to walk on. A path that transitions from flagstone to decomposed granite to a stepping stone through a groundcover planting keeps you present. You notice where you are. That noticing is what makes a walk through a garden feel like an experience rather than a commute.

## The Problem with Designing for Photos

I don't want to be hard on other designers, because I understand the pressure. Photos are how work gets found. They're how you convince a skeptical client you know what you're doing. I get it.

But I've seen the downstream effect of designing primarily for the camera, and it's not good for anyone. The client ends up with something that doesn't improve their daily life. The designer ends up with work that looks impressive but doesn't generate the kind of referrals that come from people who are genuinely happy every single day in their yard.

The deeper problem is that photogenic design tends to prioritize the single best angle, the single best moment — usually magic hour in May when everything just bloomed and the light is golden. Real gardens need to function in August at 3pm. They need to feel okay when half the plants are dormant. They need to hold up across seasons and moods and uses.

I design for the Thursday morning when you just want to sit outside before work. That's the moment I'm aiming for. Not the photo.

## How to Know Which One You're Getting

If you're talking to a designer and trying to figure out which category their work falls into, I'd ask them a few things:

- **How do you think about shade?** If the answer is purely visual — "we'll put a tree here for scale" — that's a flag. Shade is a comfort tool. It changes the temperature of a space by ten degrees. It should be placed where people are, not where it looks good in a plan.
- **What's the experience of walking through this going to be like?** If they can't describe it in sensory terms — what you'll hear, what you'll smell, how the surface will feel — they might be thinking in two dimensions.
- **What does this look like in three years?** A garden that photographs well on day one but doesn't have a plan for how it matures over time is going to feel worse and worse. The plants that look deliberately placed when they're small look abandoned when they're overgrown and nothing ties them together.

## What I'm Always Trying to Build

I want you to go outside every day. That's it. That's the whole goal.

I want you to find a reason to be in your garden in the morning, in the afternoon heat if there's enough shade to make it bearable, in the evening when everything cools down and the sages start to smell. I want your kids to want to be out there. I want it to feel genuinely different from being inside.

That requires thinking about more than how it looks. It requires thinking about how it lives.

The camera can capture a lot of things. But it can't capture what it feels like when the Santa Ana winds die down after a few rough days and you walk outside and the air is cool and still and the grasses are catching the low afternoon light and you just — stay. You don't go back in.

That's the garden I'm trying to build. Every time.
