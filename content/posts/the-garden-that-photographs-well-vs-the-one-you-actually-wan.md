There's a particular kind of garden that shows up everywhere online right now. Clean lines, matching planters, perfectly spaced succulents, decomposed granite raked smooth, maybe a single ornamental grass blowing in what appears to be a very cooperative breeze. It looks great in a photo. It looks like someone cares.

And then you stand in it, and something is missing.

I've been doing this long enough to know that the camera lies — or more accurately, the camera only captures a fraction of what a space actually *is*. It can't tell you that the flagstone gets cool under your feet after sunset. It can't tell you that the artemisia smells like sage and rain. It can't tell you that the big decomposed granite yard gives off heat like a skillet at three in the afternoon in August.

So let me talk about the gap — between what photographs well and what actually feels good — because I think it matters more than most people realize when they're planning a landscape.

## The Camera Flattens Everything

A photograph collapses three-dimensional space into two dimensions. That's obvious, but think about what that means for a garden. It means the sense of *enclosure* you feel when you step into a courtyard surrounded by tall native grasses — that quality of being held by a space — disappears completely in a photo. It means the way a path curves gently and hides what's around the bend, creating a kind of curiosity and movement, just looks like a nice path.

The gardens I'm most proud of are the ones where clients say something like, "I don't know, I just want to stay out here." That feeling almost never has a single visual explanation. It's a composition of things: scale, sound, scent, texture underfoot, the way light comes through leaves at four o'clock.

None of that shows up in a wide shot.

## Texture Is Meant to Be Touched, Not Seen

I'm a flagstone person. I'll say it plainly. And part of the reason is that flagstone has variation — no two pieces are the same thickness, the same color, the same surface. Irregular Pennsylvania bluestone, or local Bouquet Canyon, or Arizona sandstone — these materials have a physical reality to them that poured concrete just doesn't.

When you walk across well-set flagstone, you feel it. There's a slight unevenness that keeps your body engaged, keeps you present. Same reason I love decomposed granite paths with a stabilizer, set between boulders, edged with something that sprawls — like a low-growing ceanothus or a mat of Carex pansa softening the edge.

Contrast that with the stamped concrete "Tuscany pattern" that's been sealed until it's the color of a traffic cone. It photographs fine. It feels like a parking structure.

Materials that age beautifully, that accumulate a little moss in the joints, that darken when wet and lighten when dry — those materials create sensory experience over time. The camera doesn't capture time. Your body does.

## Scale and Enclosure: The Things Photos Can't Measure

Here's something I think about constantly on design jobs: the human body has a scale, and gardens should be built in relationship to that scale.

A big open yard with nothing taller than two feet is visually readable in a photo — you can see all of it, it looks clean, nothing is hidden. But stand in it and you feel exposed. There's no enclosure. Nothing draws you in or shelters you. It's a lawn, not a garden.

I like spaces that have layers. Tall stuff in the back or at the edges — maybe a Toyon or a native elderberry — then mid-height things like salvias or a big Agave, then something low and sprawling at the path edge. That layering creates rooms within a garden. It gives the eye places to rest and places to explore. It changes as you move through it.

That movement is the thing. A garden worth spending time in reveals itself slowly. You don't see it all from the door.

## The Scent Dimension That No Algorithm Can Replicate

Southern California has some of the most fragrant plants on earth, and most people have no idea. We grow things here that gardeners in other parts of the country would trade almost anything for.

A few that I use regularly and genuinely love for what they bring to a sensory experience:

- **Cleveland Sage (Salvia clevelandii)** — crushed in your hand, it smells like the hills after rain. Nothing else smells like this. Nothing.
- **Lemonadeberry (Rhus integrifolia)** — the waxy leaves have a faint, clean citrus edge. Run your hand along a hedge of it and you'll smell it.
- **Artemisia 'Canyon Gray'** — low, silver, spreads beautifully over a slope, and smells like dry California sage on a warm day.
- **Dietes or native iris edges near a path** — the leaves brushed as you walk past, releasing that subtle grassy scent.
- **White sage (Salvia apiana)** — I use this carefully, because it needs to be in the right spot. But near an entry or along a path where it gets brushed? Transcendent.

None of this comes through in a photo. A photograph of Cleveland Sage just looks like a lumpy gray-green shrub. It doesn't tell you that standing next to it on a warm evening is one of the genuine small pleasures available in this life.

## Sound Is Part of the Design

I think about acoustics in gardens more than most people expect. Not in a technical way — I'm not installing fountains by formula — but in an intuitive way.

The sound of wind moving through a Mexican feather grass (*Nassella tenuissima*) is not the same as the silence of a concrete yard. The soft percussion of water falling on river rock is not the same as water sheeting off a formal spillway into a tiled basin. Both might photograph similarly. They feel completely different.

I like rustling. I like movement. I like the sound that makes you forget, briefly, that you're inside the city.

Ornamental grasses, native bunchgrasses, tall sedges, even the papery seed heads of native buckwheat in late summer — these are acoustic elements. Design them in, not as an afterthought but as part of the whole.

## The Real Question to Ask Before You Design

If you're planning a landscape — whether you're working with someone like me or doing it yourself — I'd encourage you to ask a different question than "what do I want it to look like?"

Ask: *How do I want to feel when I'm out here?*

Do you want to feel sheltered and private? Then you need height and enclosure, not just greenery. Do you want to feel like you're somewhere wild, somewhere that doesn't look like it came from a box store? Then you need plant material with personality, stone that looks like it was found rather than manufactured, and a path that invites you to walk slowly.

Do you want to feel cool in summer, or dazzled by early morning light through a grove of trees? Those are achievable things. They just require thinking about the space as a *lived experience* rather than a visual composition.

## What I Actually Mean by Organic and Whimsical

I use these words because they're the closest I can get to describing the feeling I'm after. Not "wild" in the sense of untended. Not "whimsical" in the sense of cute or decorative.

I mean spaces that feel like they have an internal logic that came from the land, not from a catalog. Where the boulders look like they might have been there before the house was. Where the plants lean into each other the way they do on a hillside in the Santa Monicas. Where there's a slight surprise around every corner — a seat built into a retaining wall, a small clearing you didn't expect, a tree that frames a view you didn't know was there.

Those are the things that make a body want to stay.

And that's ultimately what I'm designing for. Not the photo. The staying.
