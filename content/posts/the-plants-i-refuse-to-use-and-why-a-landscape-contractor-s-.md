## Iceplant (*Carpobrotus edulis* and its cousins)

Let's start with the one that's going to ruffle some feathers.

I know. You've seen it everywhere. It covers slopes, it blooms pink and magenta in spring, and it's cheap. But iceplant is invasive. Genuinely, ecologically harmful invasive. It smothers everything beneath it, forms a dense mat that crowds out native species, and when it dies back — which it does, in patches, in a way that looks like mange — it slides off slopes and takes topsoil with it. It's not stable. It's not beautiful up close. And it does real damage to coastal sage scrub habitat.

There are better slope solutions. *Ceanothus*, *Epilobium canum*, *Eriogonum* species. Plants that belong here. I'll use any of them before I'll roll out iceplant.

## Lantana (*Lantana camara*)

This one pains me a little, because there's a native *Lantana* — *Lantana involucrata* — that I like just fine. But the common *Lantana camara* that you'll find at every box store? It's invasive in Southern California's wildland edges, it's toxic to pets and children, and it has a particular chemical quality to its smell when you brush against it that I find genuinely unpleasant. Almost medicinal. Like something you'd use to strip furniture.

Beyond all that, it belongs to the category of plants I call "catalog plants" — things that were mass-selected for color output and ease of production, not for how they interact with a real landscape. They look fine in a photo. In the ground, between other plants, they fight everything around them.

## Agapanthus (*Agapanthus africanus*)

I'll brace for impact here too.

Agapanthus is everywhere in Orange County. Every strip mall, every apartment complex, every builder-grade installation from 1985 to right now. And that's exactly the problem. Not that it's terrible — it's tough, it blooms, it tolerates neglect. But it has no spirit to it. It's completely inert as a design element. It just *sits there*, looking like it was specified from a spreadsheet, which it was.

More importantly, it's not native. It hails from South Africa. It escapes cultivation. It requires regular summer water when most of us are trying to move toward drought-tolerant landscapes. And there are so many better choices — native *Camassia*, *Muhlenbergia rigens* in bloom, *Hesperoyucca whipplei* — plants with genuine presence that tell you something about this place.

When I see a yard lined with agapanthus along the driveway, I see a missed opportunity every single time.

## Oleander (*Nerium oleander*)

Here's the practical one. Every part of oleander is toxic. Every part. The leaves, the stems, the flowers, the smoke if you burn the clippings. I won't plant something in a family yard that sends a child to the emergency room if they chew on the wrong twig. And beyond safety, it's another one that's naturalized into wild areas in California, crowding out riparian plants along dry streambeds.

It's tough, yes. It blooms, yes. But toughness and toxicity together aren't enough of an argument for me.

---

## Dwarf Fountain Grass (*Pennisetum setaceum* 'Rubrum' and related cultivars)

The purple-leafed fountain grass. You know the one. It's beautiful, genuinely — that burgundy foliage catches the afternoon light and it moves well in the breeze. I understand why people love it.

But *Pennisetum setaceum* is on California's invasive species list. The standard green species has escaped cultivation across Southern California and Hawaii and displaced native grasses across thousands of acres. The sterile purple varieties are less of a direct threat, but I stay away from the whole genus on principle, and also because I think we can do better.

Native grasses give you movement, texture, seasonal interest, and actual ecological value. *Muhlenbergia rigens* (deer grass) is magnificent — silver-green blades and tall feathery plumes. *Nassella pulchra* (purple needlegrass, our state grass) is subtle and beautiful. *Carex pansa* makes a quiet, soft groundcover. None of them are trying to take over the hillside.

---

## Sago Palm (*Cycas revoluta*)

Not a palm. Not a California plant. Extremely toxic — to pets especially, and the toxicity is severe and fast-moving. I have watched the landscaping industry sell this plant as a "low-maintenance accent" for decades, and it baffles me every time. The seeds are the most dangerous part, and they fall. They look interesting, I'll grant that. But so does a *Hesperoyucca*, so does a *Furcraea*, so does a beautiful specimen *Agave* — and none of those will kill your dog.

## The Pattern Behind All of These

If you look at this list together, you'll notice something. Most of these plants share a few traits:

- They were selected for ease and production, not for place
- They're invasive or ecologically disruptive in California
- They offer little to pollinators, birds, or the local food web
- They require more water than the land wants to give them
- They look like they came from a parts catalog rather than a living landscape

I'm not interested in landscapes that could exist anywhere. I'm interested in landscapes that could only exist here — on this specific hillside, in this specific light, in this specific microclimate with its hot afternoons and its coastal fog and its dry summers. The plants I choose are the ones that belong to that story.

The ones on this list don't. And after enough years in the ground with my hands in the soil, that's reason enough.
