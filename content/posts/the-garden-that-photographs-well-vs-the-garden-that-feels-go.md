There's a version of landscaping that looks incredible in a listing photo and feels completely hollow when you're standing in it. Symmetrical. Clean. Every plant trimmed to the same height, hardscape laid out in a perfect grid, a color palette that reads well on a screen. It photographs great. And then you walk through it and feel... nothing.

I see this constantly in Orange County. Yards designed from the outside in — from the curb, from the camera angle — rather than from the inside out. From how it feels to walk barefoot across the stone. From how the air moves through the plants on a warm afternoon. From whether there's actually a reason to be out there.

This is something I think about on every job. And I want to try to explain the difference.

## A Photo Flattens Everything a Garden Actually Is

A camera compresses depth. It erases smell, temperature, texture, and sound. It removes the feeling of shade moving across your arm or the way your foot lands differently on flagstone than on poured concrete.

What reads as beautiful in a photograph — crisp lines, high contrast, bold geometry — is not the same as what feels beautiful in person. In fact, those qualities can work against you. A perfectly symmetrical layout can feel rigid and slightly anxious to be inside of. A concrete patio with sharp 90-degree edges looks clean in a photo and feels like a parking structure when you're sitting on it at 4pm in July.

Gardens that *feel* good are designed around a different set of inputs. Things a camera can't capture.

## What the Camera Can't Capture

When I'm designing a space, I'm thinking about things like:

- **Enclosure.** Does this space feel held? Is there something above you — a canopy, a pergola draped in something alive — or are you just sitting in the open, exposed? People relax in spaces that have some sense of shelter. It's biological. A low-growing native oak eventually gives you that. A clean rectangular planter does not.

- **Texture underfoot.** Decomposed granite, irregular flagstone, loose river pebble — these materials ask your body to pay attention. They're sensory. They change how you walk. They slow you down, which is exactly what a garden should do. A perfectly uniform concrete paver field looks great in a photo and tells your nervous system you're in an office park.

- **The sound of rustling.** I put native grasses near seating areas deliberately — *Muhlenbergia rigens*, *Nassella pulchra* — because they move. They talk in the wind. You might not even consciously notice it, but it registers. It signals that something alive is happening around you. Broadleaf evergreen shrubs trimmed into spheres make no sound at all.

- **Fragrance.** Cleveland sage (*Salvia clevelandii*) smells like California chaparral. Lemon verbena smells like lemon cake. White sage on a warm afternoon is almost meditative. These are things a camera cannot show, but they're the things people describe when they talk about loving a garden.

- **Imperfection.** This one is hard to sell sometimes, but it's important. A garden that looks too perfect feels maintained rather than alive. A little wildness — a grass that seeds itself into the flagstone, a meandering boulder placement, a plant that spills past its border — these give a garden personality. They make it feel like it's been there. Perfection reads as control, and control doesn't relax people.

## The Hardscape Problem

A lot of what drives a "photo-ready" yard is expensive, polished hardscape. And I understand the appeal — it's durable, it's high contrast, it reads as premium. But I've watched people invest serious money into large-format porcelain pavers, glass tile fire features, and LED accent lighting that makes everything look like a hotel, and then never actually use the space.

Compare that to a flagstone patio set slightly irregular, surrounded by low-growing *Dymondia margaretae* filling the gaps, a few weathered boulders that came from the same geological world as the stone, a thornless native rose climbing something overhead. That space has weight. It has age, even when it's new. It draws you in rather than impressing you from a distance.

The best hardscape I've ever installed doesn't look installed. It looks like it was always there.

## The Design Moves That Make a Garden Feel Good

These aren't tricks. They're just priorities — things I put at the center of every design.

- **Layer the planting.** Low groundcovers, mid-height flowering perennials, a structural shrub or two, one or two small trees. Layers create depth that you *feel* around you, not just see.
- **Create a reason to walk.** A path that curves slightly, a stepping stone that leads somewhere, a view that reveals itself as you move through the space. Movement is part of what makes a garden feel alive.
- **Put seating somewhere slightly hidden.** Not front and center on the patio facing the house. Tuck a chair or a bench into a corner, under something, facing outward. People sit in those spots. They linger.
- **Use materials that age well.** Flagstone, boulders, steel, wood — things that get more beautiful as they weather. Not things that need to be pressure-washed back to their original state every year to look right.
- **Choose plants that move.** Grasses, wispy perennials, anything that responds to the wind. A static planting is a photo. A garden that moves is a living thing.

## The Honest Trade-Off

I'll be direct with you: a garden designed to feel good doesn't always photograph the same way a catalog garden does. It might look a little less polished. The lines might not be as crisp. There might be a plant leaning into a path, or a boulder that isn't centered, or a grass that's doing its own thing.

But you'll use it. You'll sit in it. You'll walk through it without your shoes and feel the warmth the flagstone held from the afternoon sun. You'll smell the sage when the marine layer rolls in. You'll hear the grasses move in the offshore wind and feel something loosen in your chest.

That's what a garden is for. Not for the photo. For that.
