There's a moment I've noticed on jobs — usually about ten minutes after a client has walked through a finished space for the first time. The photos are done. The phone goes back in the pocket. And then they just... stand there. Sometimes they don't say anything at all. That silence is what I'm always working toward.

It's different from the gasp you get when someone sees a stunning photograph of a landscape. That gasp is about recognition — *oh, that's beautiful.* The silence is something deeper. It's the body saying *I want to stay here.*

Those are two very different things, and the landscaping industry has spent the last decade optimizing almost entirely for the first one.

## Why Photogenic Gardens Often Disappoint in Person

A photograph flattens everything. It removes smell, temperature, sound, the feeling of gravel shifting under your feet, the way a breeze moves through a stand of deer grass at dusk. It turns a three-dimensional, living experience into a single plane of light and color.

So when a garden is designed primarily to photograph well — clean lines, bold contrasts, dramatic focal points, lots of hardscape — it tends to deliver exactly what it promises in two dimensions. The problem is you have to live in three.

I see this constantly in Orange County. Outdoor kitchens with perfect stone veneer and not a lick of shade. Pools rimmed with the same bullnose travertine that's been on every pool deck since 2008. Sod lawns so evenly green they look like felt. Raised planters with succulents arranged in perfect rosette spirals. Specimen plants installed as sculpture, not as living things. It all photographs beautifully. It photographs exactly like the Houzz board the client emailed me.

And then you sit in it on a Sunday afternoon in July and you feel... nothing. Or worse, you feel exposed and vaguely bored.

## What "Feeling Good" Actually Requires

When I start a design, I'm not thinking about what it'll look like in photos. I'm thinking about what it'll feel like at 6pm on a warm evening when you finally get to exhale. I'm thinking about what it'll smell like in February when the *Salvia clevelandii* starts to wake up. I'm thinking about whether there's somewhere to sit that feels enclosed enough to feel private but open enough to feel like you're still part of the outdoors.

That's a different set of questions entirely.

Here's what I've found actually creates that feeling:

- **Enclosure.** Not walls, necessarily — but the sense that the space has edges. A garden with one tall native shrub mass on the north side, a low stone seat wall on the south, and open sky above it can feel like a room. Without that enclosure, you're just standing in a yard.

- **Texture you can hear and touch.** Decomposed granite that crunches underfoot. Boulders with rough, honest surfaces. Grasses that make a soft hushing sound when the air moves. These things register in the body before the brain even processes them.

- **Shade that makes sense.** Not an umbrella bolted to a table. I mean a *Cercis occidentalis* with its broad canopy throwing dappled shade over a flagstone seating area, or a pergola draped in *Distictis buccinatoria* that filters afternoon sun into something gold and gentle. The kind of shade that changes with the time of day and the season.

- **A reason to move through the space.** A path that curves slightly, not because that's a rule of good design, but because curving paths make you slow down. A garden you have to walk through to discover is a garden worth being in.

- **Plants that are doing something.** Not performing, not posing — actually growing, attracting, responding. A *Salvia 'Bee's Bliss'* spilling over a flagstone edge, covered in bees in April, is worth ten drought-tolerant specimen plants staked like sculptures in a gravel field.

## The Problem With Designing for the Photo

I'm not against good design photography. I'm glad when a finished space photographs well — it usually means we got the composition right and the proportions honest. But there's a trap in starting with the visual.

When you start with how something looks, you design surfaces. When you start with how something feels, you design *experiences.*

Surfaces photograph beautifully. Experiences don't translate. You can't photograph the way a boulder radiates warmth at 7pm after a sunny day, or the smell of *Artemisia californica* after the first rain, or the way your shoulders drop when you sit in a chair that's in exactly the right spot — backed up against something solid, facing something open.

Those are the things people remember. Those are the things that make someone say, three years after a project is done, *I live in that garden.*

## What I Actually Look for When I Walk a Site

Before I draw anything, I spend time on a site just being in it. Where does the afternoon shade fall? Where does the wind come from? Is there a view worth framing, or one worth blocking? Where would a person naturally want to sit — not because I'm going to put a chair there, but because understanding that instinct tells me something true about the land.

I'm looking for the bones. The things that, if I work with them rather than against them, will give the finished space that quality of inevitability — like it was always supposed to be there.

That quality almost never comes from a catalog. It comes from listening to what the site is already telling you.

## A Note on Native Plants and Realness

Part of why I work almost exclusively with California native and Mediterranean-climate plants is that they carry that quality with them naturally. A *Muhlenbergia rigens* planted in the right spot — in full sun, backed by a warm-toned boulder, room to arch — looks like it grew there because it wanted to. It moves. It changes. It smells like something after rain. It has a season, a story.

Compare that to the kind of ornamental grass you find in a landscape supply catalog: uniform, consistent, bred for visual impact, planted in rows. It looks great in the first photo of the project. Then it just... sits there.

Plants that belong to the land make gardens that feel like they belong to the land. It's that simple, and it took me years to understand just how non-negotiable that is for me.

## The Bottom Line

If you're planning a landscape project, I'd ask you to hold two questions at once: *Does this look beautiful?* and *Does this feel like somewhere I actually want to be?*

If you can only answer yes to the first one, keep designing.

The goal isn't a garden that performs for the camera. It's a garden that earns that silence — the one where you put your phone away, sit down, and forget to think about anything else for a while.

That's the one worth building.
