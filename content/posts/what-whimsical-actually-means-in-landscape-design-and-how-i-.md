There's a word I use a lot when I talk to people about their yards: *whimsical*. And almost every time I say it, I watch someone's face do the same thing — a small flinch, like they're trying to decide if I just described something they love or something they're afraid of.

So let me clear it up, because it's the most important word in how I think about landscape design, and it gets misunderstood constantly.

Whimsical does not mean messy. It does not mean overgrown, or random, or cute in a gnome-and-fairy-door kind of way. What it means — the way I mean it — is that the landscape feels like it arrived. Like it has always been there, or like it could have grown there on its own if the land had a particular kind of luck. It means the eye moves through the space and finds something unexpected, then something that makes sense, then something beautiful. In that order, ideally more than once.

It means the space has a pulse.

Getting there isn't an accident. It's one of the more technically demanding things I do.

## Why "Perfectly Designed" Landscapes Feel Wrong

Spend an afternoon walking through a nursery that caters to tract home builders, or scroll through certain landscape contractor websites, and you'll see the opposite of what I'm describing. Matching shrubs in matching mounds. Concrete edging in perfect arcs. Three of the same ornamental grass, evenly spaced, like soldiers.

It's not bad, exactly. It's just dead.

Nature doesn't work in repetition and symmetry. Look at a California hillside in March — the way black sage grows in loose drifts, buckwheat spills over a rock, a single toyon catches the light at the top of a slope. There's structure in it, a clear logic, but nothing is mirrored or gridded. The intelligence is hidden. That's the target.

When a landscape looks too composed, too deliberate, it creates a subtle tension — your brain registers that something has been controlled, and control, in a garden, is the enemy of wonder.

## The Architecture First: Why Bones Matter More Than Plants

Here's something I say to almost everyone I work with early on: the plants are the last decision we make, not the first.

Before we talk about what grows in the ground, we talk about what the ground itself is doing. Grade changes. Where the eye lands from the back door. Where someone will naturally want to walk. What the view is from the kitchen window at seven in the morning.

Whimsy needs structure underneath it, or it falls apart. Think of it like jazz — you can play freely and unexpectedly because everyone in the band knows the key. In landscape design, the key is the hardscape.

I use flagstone more than almost any other material. Decomposed granite. Boulders. Dry-stacked stone. These materials have their own internal logic — they were shaped by pressure and time, they have weight and grain and variation — and when you work with them honestly, they do a lot of the whimsy work for you. A flagstone path doesn't want to be straight. Let it curve. Let the joints be wide enough for creeping thyme to grow into them. Let one stone be larger than the others where it turns.

That's the architecture of surprise. It happens at the level of material choice before a single plant goes in.

## How I Actually Achieve the Look in Practice

Let me get specific, because philosophy without technique is just talk.

**Odd numbers and uneven groupings.** I rarely plant in twos or fours. Groups of three, five, seven — but not arranged in a row. Arranged like they seeded themselves, because they're trying to look like they did.

**Layer the height, not just the species.** A section of planting should have something low and sprawling, something mid-height with texture, and something taller that catches wind. When the wind moves through it, the layers move at different rates. That's alive. That's the feeling.

**One thing that doesn't fit — on purpose.** A large decomposed granite boulder in the middle of a soft planting bed. A California sycamore allowed to lean slightly toward the light rather than staked into submission. A path that jogs slightly left for no reason you can articulate but every reason you can feel. I'll put something in almost every design that slightly surprises you, something you weren't expecting, and that surprise is the hook. It's what you remember.

**Edges that breathe.** I almost never use hard edging between a planting bed and a path. I want California fescue to drape over a flagstone. I want a Cleveland sage to push its branches forward until you have to brush it with your arm as you walk by — and smell it, because that's the whole point. Hard edges say: these two things are separate. Soft edges say: this is one place.

**Restraint in color.** Loud, multi-colored planting schemes feel like a quilt somebody threw on the ground. I tend to work in a limited palette — a lot of silver and gray from natives like artemisia and woolly blue curls, deep greens from toyon and coffeeberry, punctuated by moments of color. One California poppy orange among all that gray is a revelation. Seven different flower colors in a four-foot bed is a headache.

## The Plants That Do This Naturally

I work almost exclusively with California natives and Mediterranean-climate plants, and part of why I love them is that they naturally lean toward the look I'm describing. They didn't evolve to be tidy.

- **Deer grass (*Muhlenbergia rigens*)** — cascades like water, moves in the lightest breeze, goes gold in fall. It looks like it's thinking.
- **Cleveland sage (*Salvia clevelandii*)** — silver-gray foliage, a bloom spike that appears like a small miracle in late spring, fragrance that hits you from eight feet away on a warm evening.
- **Toyon (*Heteromeles arbutifolia*)** — the plant California was arguably named for. Glossy leaves, red berries in winter, structure that reads like a small wild tree. Nothing catalog about it.
- **Ceanothus** — There are dozens of varieties and I use them constantly. The way they bloom in February and March, when almost nothing else does, is exactly the kind of surprise I'm building toward.
- **Flannel bush (*Fremontodendron californicum*)** — if you've seen one in bloom, you know. If you haven't, I can't adequately describe it. Find one.

These plants aren't performing. They're just being what they are, and what they are is beautiful, and that distinction matters more than I can explain in a sentence.

## The Honest Caveat

I'll be straight with you: this kind of design takes more skill, not less, than the symmetrical catalog approach. Anyone can plant three matched boxwoods in front of a window and call it done. Creating something that looks effortless — that looks like it *arrived* — requires you to understand how plants grow over time, how materials age, how the light changes through the seasons, and where the eye wants to rest.

It also takes a homeowner who trusts the process long enough to see it come together. These landscapes look better at three years than they do at three months. A lot of what I'm planting wants to sprawl and fill and find its shape. You have to give it permission.

When it works — and it does work — you'll walk out your back door in the evening, and the sage will be cooling in the air, and the grasses will be moving, and the flagstone will glow warm in the last light, and it won't look like something you bought. It will look like something that grew.

That's what whimsical means. That's what I'm building toward every time.
