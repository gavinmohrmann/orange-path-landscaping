Let me be upfront with you: I'm not anti-lawn on principle. I'm anti-lawn in the wrong place, for the wrong reasons, maintained the wrong way, in a climate that never asked for it in the first place.

In Southern California, that's most lawns.

I've been designing and building landscapes out here for a long time, and one of the most common conversations I have — still, even now — starts with a client pointing to their backyard and saying, "We're thinking grass." And I always pause before I respond, because I want to give them a real answer, not a reflexive one.

Here's what I actually think, and why.

## Lawn Is a Product, Not a Landscape

There's something worth naming right away: a lawn is essentially a manufactured surface. It's a monoculture — one species, repeated indefinitely, kept artificially uniform through constant inputs of water, fertilizer, herbicide, and mechanical force. That's a lot of work and a lot of chemistry just to make something look like a carpet.

The irony is that most lawns don't even feel alive. They look green, but they're quiet. No bees move through them. No birds hunt in them. No scent rises off them in the evening. Walk across a meadow of native bunch grasses and low-flowering groundcovers and it's a completely different sensory experience — there's movement, there's texture, there's life at every layer. A lawn, by contrast, is basically a green parking lot.

That's not a small distinction. It's the whole thing.

## The Water Argument Is Real — But It's Not Even My Main Point

Yes, turf is thirsty. A traditional Kentucky bluegrass or fescue lawn in Orange County can use 50 to 70 inches of supplemental water per year. Our annual rainfall is maybe 12 to 15 inches in a good year. The math is not subtle.

But honestly, I don't lead with water when clients push back. Water is obvious, and clients have heard it. What I want them to feel is the opportunity cost — what they're giving up by paving their yard in green.

A well-designed native garden in the same footprint can be extraordinary. I'm talking about the smell of Cleveland sage after a light rain. The way deer grass catches afternoon light and turns to something almost golden. A decomposed granite path weaving through ceanothus in full bloom — that deep, improbable blue that makes you stop mid-conversation. Black-eyed Susans threading through California fescue. A single coast live oak that, in twenty years, will give your grandchildren somewhere to sit in the shade and think.

None of that happens on a lawn.

## What I Say When Clients Push Back

The pushback usually comes in a few predictable forms, and I take each one seriously.

**"But the kids need somewhere to play."**

This is a fair point, and it's the one time I'll actually say yes to a lawn — a small one, purpose-built, in the right spot. What I push back on is making the entire yard a lawn just because you have kids. Give the children a real play area: a generous patch of soft turf or decomposed granite, maybe a simple climbing structure, a sandpit with a good boulder next to it for scrambling. Surround it with something that actually grows. The kids will use the whole yard, and so will you.

**"I just want something low maintenance."**

I hear this constantly, and I understand it — but I want to gently challenge the assumption that lawn is low maintenance. It isn't. It never is. It's just *familiar* maintenance. Weekly mowing, edging, blowing, aerating, overseeding, fertilizing, pest control. That's not low-effort. That's just what people are used to paying for.

A well-established native garden after year two or three? You're out there a few times a year. You're doing a light cutback in late winter. You're watching things you planted come back bigger and more beautiful than the year before. That's what low maintenance actually looks like.

**"I like the way a lawn looks."**

This one I respect. Aesthetics matter. But I'll ask a follow-up: what specifically do you like about it? Usually the answer is about openness, visual calm, a sense of order. And those things are achievable in a dozen other ways that don't require a constant lifeline of water and chemicals.

A wide flagstone terrace with native groundcovers spilling between the joints feels open. A designed meadow with a clear path through the center feels ordered and intentional. A simple palette of three or four well-placed plants — repeated, allowed to grow — has a quietness to it that's just as restful as turf. Sometimes more.

## What I Recommend Instead

This is where it gets interesting, because there's no single answer. The right alternative depends on:

- **How the space is used.** Entertaining? Quiet morning coffee? Active play? The function drives the design.
- **Sun and soil conditions.** We work with what's there before we decide what goes in.
- **How much visual connection there is to the interior.** If you're looking at this space from your kitchen every morning, it needs to feel like something worth looking at.
- **Budget.** Good flagstone, quality boulders, and established plants cost money upfront. But they last.

Some of my favorite combinations for lawn replacement in Southern California:

- **Decomposed granite or stabilized DG** as a base surface, with native bunchgrasses and low flowering perennials threaded through. It reads like a dry streambed or a meadow path, and it handles foot traffic well.
- **Flagstone with creeping groundcover in the joints** — plants like dymondia, creeping thyme, or woolly thyme that can take light foot traffic and smell incredible underfoot.
- **A mix of mulched planting beds and open hardscape** — gravel, boulders, a small patio — with the plants doing the visual work and the hardscape giving you usable surface.
- **Native grasses as a lawn alternative** — UC Verde buffalo grass or no-mow fescue blends can work in the right conditions and give you that "soft green carpet" look at a fraction of the water.

## The Bigger Picture

I think what I'm really saying is this: lawn is a default, and defaults deserve scrutiny. Most people ask for grass not because it's the best solution for their yard, but because it's what they grew up with, what their neighbors have, what they know.

My job is to show them something better. Something that belongs here — in this climate, on this land, in this light. Something that grows more interesting with every season instead of demanding the same effort to stay the same.

That's the kind of landscape I want to build. And honestly, once clients see it in person — once they walk through it, smell it, watch a hummingbird work through the salvia — most of them never look back.
