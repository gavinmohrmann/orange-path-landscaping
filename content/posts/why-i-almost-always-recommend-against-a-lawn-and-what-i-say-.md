Let me be upfront about something: I don't hate grass. I grew up running barefoot across it like everyone else. What I have is a measured, honest opinion about where it belongs — and in most Southern California residential yards, the answer is nowhere.

That's a strong position, and I know it. So let me walk you through how I think about this.

## The Problem Isn't Aesthetic. It's Systemic.

When someone asks me about putting in a lawn, my first question is always the same: *what do you actually need this space to do?*

Because lawn, as a surface, has one real job. It's soft underfoot, it's visually uniform, and it invites people to lie down or kick a ball around. That's it. That's the whole resume.

And for that one narrow function, it demands an extraordinary amount in return. We're talking weekly mowing, regular edging, irrigation running two to three times a week through summer, fertilizer, aeration, overseeding, pesticide if you want it to stay green and not get chewed up by grubs or chinch bugs. It's a maintenance contract you sign with the land every single season, forever, until you decide you're done and rip the whole thing out.

In a state that's been in some form of drought for most of the last two decades, that trade-off is hard to justify. I'm not saying this to lecture anyone — I'm saying it because I watch homeowners get tired of lawn. I see it on jobs all the time. The drip system breaks in July and nobody notices until half the turf is dead. The dog digs a trench along the fence line. The shade from the new patio cover kills the corner section. Lawn is unforgiving of neglect in a way that native plantings simply aren't.

## What Clients Say When They Push Back

Here are the three things I hear most often, and what I actually tell people in response.

**"My kids need somewhere to play."**

This is a legitimate reason to want some kind of lawn — but notice how small that footprint actually needs to be. A ten-by-twenty patch of turf, tucked into the right corner of the yard and bordered by decomposed granite or flagstone, can give you a real play surface without demanding that you sacrifice your entire outdoor space to its upkeep. I'll often suggest a durable turf variety like Zoysia or a fine fescue blend that holds up to foot traffic with less water than a standard bluegrass mix. Or we look at synthetic turf in that specific zone — which I'm not wild about aesthetically, but which makes real sense for a high-use, defined play area where the kids are actually spending time.

What I won't do is design a full backyard of lawn around a hypothetical of two kids who might kick a ball around sometimes. I've seen too many of those lawns become a chore that nobody loves.

**"I want something low maintenance."**

Lawn is not low maintenance. I don't know how this idea persists, but it does, and I address it directly. A well-designed native garden — Salvia, Achillea, Muhlenbergia, Encelia, Cistus, Lantana, ornamental grasses, a few well-placed boulders — will take some time to establish in year one and almost nothing after that. Once the root systems are down, you're trimming back a few times a year and enjoying it the rest of the time. The colors shift with the seasons. The birds show up. The whole thing starts to feel alive in a way that a flat plane of turf never does.

That's not a romantic pitch. That's what happens.

**"The HOA or the previous owner had one, so I assumed I should too."**

This one I understand most of all. We inherit landscapes the way we inherit furniture — without really questioning whether it fits. But convention isn't design. If a lawn was installed because it was cheap and familiar in 1985, that's not a reason to keep replacing it in 2025.

## What I Suggest Instead

This depends entirely on the site — the soil, the sun exposure, the microclimate, the way the space connects to the house — but there are a handful of combinations I come back to again and again in Orange County yards.

- **Decomposed granite with flagstone pathways and native plantings**: DG has a warmth to it, especially the gold or buff-toned varieties, and it reads as ground cover without reading as hardscape. It's permeable. It smells like dry California hills after a rain. I lay it in generous sweeps and let plants spill over the edges.

- **Ornamental native grasses**: Muhlenbergia rigens — deer grass — is extraordinary. It moves in the wind like something from a Japanese ink painting. Nassella pulchra, purple needlegrass, is California's state grass for a reason. These don't need mowing. They need occasional cutting back and that's about it.

- **Low groundcovers**: Dymondia margaretae between flagstone steps, Myoporum parvifolium spilling over a grade change, Carex pansa in a part-shade corner. These are soft on the eye and genuinely simple to maintain once established.

- **Hardscape with intention**: A good patio — real flagstone, not the poured concrete slabs with a stamped pattern — gives you a living space that never needs to be watered and never turns brown in August. I've seen homeowners fall in love with their yards for the first time after we pull out the lawn and lay in a proper patio with a fire element, some potted natives, and lights in the right places.

## The Honest Summary

Lawn isn't immoral. It's just usually wrong for the site, wrong for the climate, and wrong for the life the homeowner actually wants to be living.

When I sit down with someone who wants a lawn, I'm not trying to talk them out of their vision. I'm trying to get to what's underneath it — the feeling they want when they walk outside, the way they want to use the space, the amount of time they're genuinely willing to give to it. And when we get honest about all of that, we almost always end up somewhere better than grass.

The best landscapes I've designed feel like the land made them. Like the plants decided to grow there, like the stones were always present, like the whole thing exhaled slowly over a long California afternoon and arrived at exactly this arrangement. That's what I'm after. And a lawn, beautiful as it can be in the right context, almost never gets you there.
