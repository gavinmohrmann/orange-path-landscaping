There's a moment at the beginning of every project — we're standing in the backyard, maybe the sun is cutting through an old oak, maybe the soil smells like dry clay and eucalyptus — and before I say anything about plants or pavers or drainage, I ask this:

**"How do you actually *use* this space right now?"**

Not how you want to use it. Not what you've seen on Pinterest. How you use it *today*.

And nine times out of ten, the answer is some version of: "Honestly? We don't."

That answer is everything.

---

## Why the Honest Answer Matters More Than the Dream Answer

Most people come to me with a vision — a mood board, a few Instagram saves, maybe something they saw at a hotel in Palm Springs. And I love that. I want to know what moves you aesthetically. But if I designed around your dream without understanding your reality, I'd be building a stage set. Something that looks beautiful in photos and sits unused eleven months out of the year.

The spaces that actually get lived in? They were designed around *behavior*, not aspiration.

When someone tells me they don't use their backyard, I start listening for *why*. Is it too hot in the afternoon? No shade, no place to sit that feels sheltered? Is it visually chaotic — too much going on, no focal point, nowhere for the eye to rest? Is it just... unfinished? Like a room with no furniture, so nobody ever goes in?

Those are all fixable problems. But they're different problems, and they need different solutions.

---

## What the Follow-Up Questions Reveal

After the first question opens the door, I walk through it:

- **Do you have kids or dogs, or are you planning to?** This shapes everything from ground cover to plant selection to whether a boulder grouping can live near a pathway.
- **Do you eat outside?** A lot? Sometimes? Never? This tells me whether a dining area is a centerpiece or a footnote.
- **Are you a gardener, or do you want to set it and mostly forget it?** I ask this gently, because people often feel embarrassed to say they don't want to do much maintenance. There's no wrong answer. But it dramatically affects what we plant and how we structure the irrigation.
- **What time of day do you picture yourself out here?** Morning coffee people want something soft and cool-toned, dappled light, maybe the sound of a small water feature. Evening people want warmth — good lighting, ambient heat if we're doing it right, something that glows.
- **What do you want to feel when you walk out that door?**

That last one. That's where it gets interesting.

---

## "I Want It to Feel Like It Belongs Here"

I hear this more than anything else, and it's my favorite answer to work with.

When someone says they want their yard to feel like it *belongs* — not like a showroom, not like a resort, not like something dropped onto the land from somewhere else — that tells me they're ready to do this the right way.

That's the person I can talk to about California natives. About how a Toyon or a Cleveland Sage doesn't just look right in a Southern California garden — it *is* right, because it evolved here, in this soil, under this sun, with these dry summers and these Santa Ana winds. It doesn't need to be coaxed. It just needs to be placed.

That's the person who gets excited when I talk about dry-stacked flagstone instead of poured concrete — about how the irregular edges of a Santa Barbara sandstone path invite your eye to slow down, how the texture of it under your feet feels like something real rather than something manufactured.

That's the person who understands why I'd rather find one beautiful lichen-covered boulder at a local quarry and make it the heart of a planting bed than install a catalog fountain that looks the same in every yard in the neighborhood.

---

## When the Answer Is More Complicated

Sometimes the answer to "how do you use this space?" is surprisingly specific. A couple who does tai chi in the morning. A family with three kids under ten who need room to move. Someone who works from home and wants the yard to be visible from their desk — a living painting they can look at while they're on calls.

Each of those unlocks a completely different design language.

The tai chi couple gets a flat, open plane with soft gravel or decomposed granite, surrounded by low native grasses that move in the morning air — *Muhlenbergia rigens*, maybe, or *Nassella pulchra* — something that responds to the slightest breeze. Calm, minimal, intentional.

The family with young kids gets flexibility. Resilient groundcovers. Enough open lawn or DG to kick a ball around. Plants that aren't going to be a hazard — no spiky agaves at face height, nothing toxic. Boulders set low and used as seating, not obstacles. Room to grow into the space as the kids do.

The person who wants a living painting gets a design built around sightlines. I'll walk through the house, stand at that desk, and design backward from that window frame. What you see first matters. What your eye travels to matters. The composition of a garden is no different than the composition of a painting — it has a foreground, a middle ground, a horizon.

---

## The Deeper Truth Behind the Question

Here's what I've learned after years of doing this: most people don't know what they want from their outdoor space until someone asks them the right question.

They know they want it to look better. They know they're tired of the dead grass or the cracked concrete or the builder-grade shrubs that have never looked quite right. But the deeper want — the thing underneath the aesthetic — that's usually about *feeling* something.

Calm. Restored. At home. Like the world slowed down for a minute.

A well-designed yard can do that. Not because of the plants or the stone or the lighting, exactly — but because all of those things were chosen *together*, for *this* person, for *this* piece of land, in response to how they actually live.

That's why the first question matters.

The design doesn't start with a plant palette or a budget or a style. It starts with an honest conversation about a life. Everything else follows from there.
