Southern California fools a lot of people. The climate is so generous that you can throw almost anything in the ground and get something pretty by June. Bougainvillea goes crazy, salvias bloom their heads off, the Mexican sage is a purple cloud. Everything looks intentional, even when it isn't.

Then December comes.

The annuals are done. The bougainvillea drops its color. The garden that looked lush and composed six months ago looks like a collection of sticks and brown patches held together by good lighting. That's when you find out whether you actually *designed* something — or just planted it.

I've been doing this work long enough to know that the gardens I'm most proud of look different in winter, but they never look empty. There's always something holding the space together. That's not an accident. It's the result of thinking in layers, thinking in seasons, and making intentional choices about structure before you ever put a single plant in the ground.

Here's how I approach it.

---

## Start With the Bones

Before you think about flowers, think about what's going to be there when the flowers aren't.

Structure in a garden comes from a few things: hardscape, evergreen plants, and what I call *architectural plants* — things with such a strong form that they read clearly even when they're not doing anything showy.

I spend a lot of time thinking about hardscape as part of the living composition of a garden. Flagstone paths that weave through plantings, a dry-stacked boulder grouping, a decomposed granite sitting area edged in steel — these things don't go dormant. They don't have an off-season. They're doing their work in January just as much as in May, and in Southern California's low-angle winter light, natural stone especially tends to look *better* — warmer, more textured, more honest.

If your garden's structure only exists in the plants, you're one bad season away from nothing.

---

## Anchor With Evergreen Natives

This is where California's native palette earns its keep in a way that's deeply underappreciated.

Think about what Toyon looks like in December — loaded with red berries, deep green foliage, looking more alive than most things in a conventional garden. Or a well-placed Ceanothus that's been allowed to grow into its natural arching form. Or Coffeeberry, with its dark stems and subtle fruit. These plants don't need to be coaxed through winter. Winter is just another season to them. They've been here far longer than we have.

When I'm designing a planting plan, I make sure there's a backbone of evergreen structure — usually 40 to 50 percent of the plant palette — that holds the space together through the cold months. These are the plants that define the edges of a path, frame a view, give mass to a corner. Everything else layers in around them.

Some of my go-to structural evergreens for Southern California:

- **Toyon (Heteromeles arbutifolia)** — Brilliant December berries, tough as nails, birds love it
- **Lemonade Berry (Rhus integrifolia)** — Dense, dark, almost sculptural, coastal or inland
- **Coffeeberry (Frangula californica)** — Refined and understated, incredible variety between cultivars
- **Cleveland Sage (Salvia clevelandii)** — Semi-evergreen, aromatic, holds form well in winter
- **Deer Grass (Muhlenbergia rigens)** — The backbone grass, glows in winter light, never looks tired

---

## Think in Waves, Not Moments

A lot of garden design gets taught as if you're designing a photograph — one perfect moment, one ideal season. That's the wrong way to think about it.

I think about a garden in waves. What's the first thing that wakes up in late winter? What carries June? What's doing something interesting in September when everything else is heat-stressed? What gives you form and color in December when you're walking to your car in the morning and you want the garden to say something to you?

You don't need every plant to be interesting in every month. You need *something* to be interesting in every month, and you need the things that are resting to rest gracefully.

This is where I get opinionated about ornamental grasses. A well-grown clump of Muhlenbergia capillaris — pink muhly grass — by mid-October is one of the most spectacular things in a Southern California garden. A billowing cloud of fine pink plumes catching the afternoon light. And in December, when it's gone to seed, the dried stalks still have movement and texture and catch the dew in the morning in a way that feels more alive than a lot of "green" plants do.

Dead and dormant doesn't mean ugly. It can mean sculptural. But you have to choose the right plants for that to be true.

---

## Use Contrast Deliberately

One of the things that makes a garden feel composed — not just planted — is contrast. And contrast reads most clearly in winter, when you're not being distracted by flowers.

Fine-textured plants against coarse-textured ones. Upright forms against mounding ones. Dark foliage against silver foliage. This is the grammar of garden design, and it needs to be working even when nothing is in bloom.

I love pairing the silver filigree of a woolly blue curls (Trichostema lanatum) against the bold paddle leaves of a low-growing agave. Or running a sweep of fine-textured deer grass along a path edged in chunky, irregular flagstone. The contrast is doing work. It's giving your eye something to travel across and linger on, even on a gray December morning.

Color can be a crutch. When color goes away in winter, contrast is what's left. Design for it on purpose.

---

## Let the Hardscape Carry Its Weight

I'll say this plainly: if you're relying on plants alone to give your garden structure, you're working too hard and spending too much money on replacement plants every few years.

Good hardscape — real flagstone, weathered boulders, hand-laid DG, reclaimed wood — has permanence. It develops character as it weathers. Moss creeps into the joints of a flagstone path and makes it look like it's been there for decades. A boulder that's been in the ground for a few years starts to feel like it grew there.

That's what I'm after. Not manufactured perfection, but something that looks like it belongs to the land it's sitting on.

In winter, when plants are quieter, hardscape speaks louder. A beautiful stone path through a bare-looking native planting is still beautiful. It gives you something to walk on, something to look at, something to frame the plants that are resting. It's the architecture of the garden, and architecture doesn't need to bloom.

---

## The December Test

When I'm evaluating a design — whether it's one I drew on paper or one I'm standing in on a job site — I do what I call the December test.

I mentally strip out every flower. Every annual. Every plant that's going to go dormant or get cut back. I ask: what's left? Is there something to look at? Is there a sense of enclosure, of movement, of depth? Does the hardscape feel like it belongs? Are the bones strong?

If the answer is yes, I know the design will work year-round. The June version will be spectacular — the layers will fill in, the salvias will bloom, the grasses will feather out in the breeze. But the December version will have quiet dignity.

That's what I'm always building toward. A garden that's stunning when everything is happening, and still says something when it isn't.

---

That balance — exuberance and restraint, abundance and structure — is what separates a garden that's designed from one that's just planted. It takes intention. It takes knowing your plants, knowing your materials, and being honest with yourself about what you're actually building.

But when you get it right, it holds up through every season. And in January, when the light is low and everything is still and the stone path is damp and the deer grass is glowing silver and the Toyon berries are going orange — that's when you really know you did something.
