The first thing I do when I walk a new property isn't stand at the curb and squint at the front yard. I go inside.

I ask the homeowner to show me where they spend time — the kitchen, the primary bedroom, the living room with the big sliding door that opens to the patio. I look out every window that faces the garden. I notice what the light does, what the sightlines are, where the eye naturally wants to land. Sometimes I stand there for a while without saying much.

People think that's strange. It isn't.

## The Street View Is a Lie

Curb appeal is a real estate term, and it's useful in exactly one context: selling a house. For everyone actually living there, the view from the street is something you experience for about fifteen seconds a day — walking to your car in the morning, walking back from it at night.

The view from your kitchen window? You might see that a thousand times a year.

So why do so many landscape designs get built around impressing people driving by at 25 miles per hour? Because it's the easy answer. It's what photos look like. It's what gets pinned on mood boards. It looks good in a presentation.

But it's not design. It's decoration.

## What the Inside View Tells Me

When I'm standing at your window, I'm reading the space like a story that hasn't been written yet. A few things I'm always looking for:

- **Where does the eye land first?** There's always a focal point waiting to happen — a corner, a slope, a gap in the fence line. If I can place something beautiful there — a sculptural boulder, a drifting clump of deer grass, a small native tree with good branching — that's the thing you'll see every morning without even trying.

- **What's the light doing?** Afternoon light coming through a west-facing window will backlight ornamental grasses and make them glow like torches. Morning light from the east catches dew and brings out the silver in plants like Cleveland sage or white sage. The view changes depending on when you're looking, and good design accounts for that.

- **What's moving?** A garden that has no movement is a painting. Wind through feather grass, a hummingbird working a monkeyflower, the way a California fuchsia shakes when something brushes past it — these are the things that make a garden feel alive. I want to know what direction the breeze typically comes from so I can plant accordingly.

- **What's the frame?** Windows are literal frames. Doors are frames. A sliding glass door is a living painting you walk past twenty times a day. I think about what I'm putting inside that frame, not just what the garden looks like from above on a site plan.

## The Inside-Out Approach Changes Everything

Once I start designing from the inside out, the priorities shift in ways that might surprise you.

A side yard that's completely invisible from the street but perfectly framed by a bedroom window? That might deserve the most thoughtful planting of the whole property. I've seen homeowners fall in love with parts of their garden they'd barely noticed before, simply because we put something worth looking at in the right spot.

And the front yard — the one everyone worries about — usually benefits from this approach too. Instead of a symmetrical, sign-of-the-times planting that photographs well and feels like nothing, we create something that has depth and interest when seen from the front door threshold. You walk out of your house into it, not just past it.

## What This Looks Like in Practice

Say you have a breakfast nook with a window that looks out to the back corner of the yard — a forgotten space with an old block wall and a overgrown photinia that nobody planted on purpose. Most designers ignore that corner. I don't.

That might be where I set a single large fieldstone boulder with a low drift of blue-eyed grass at its base and a young toyon planted just behind it, knowing in four years the toyon will get eight feet tall and develop that beautiful red berry display every winter, right there in your sightline while you drink your coffee.

That's not a big gesture. It doesn't cost much. But it means something every single day.

## The Philosophy Underneath All of It

I believe a garden should serve the people who live with it — not the people who drive past it, not the listing photos, not the Instagram grid. The best gardens I've ever walked through feel like they belong to the person who lives there. They have a particular quality that's hard to name but easy to feel: intimacy.

That quality starts with knowing where someone actually stands, what they actually see, what they actually need their landscape to give them day after day.

So yes, I'll come inside before I ever look at your yard. That's where the real design starts.
